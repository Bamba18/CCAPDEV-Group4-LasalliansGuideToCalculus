The vision and purpose behind The Lasallian's Guide to Calculus

Welcome to The Lasallian's Guide to Calculus (or TLGC for short)! We aim to provide an accompanying guide for calculus classes for students of a variety of courses; including but not limited to mathematics, statistics, computer science, engineering, physics, chemistry, and other STEM fields.

Many popular resources already exist, such as Paul’s Online Notes and various classic textbooks by Leithold, Stewart, Simmons, and more. (In fact, Simmons’ book is used as a reference for our website). However, we aim to provide two core elements that other sources either do not have or do not cover in as much breadth:

  1. Interactive visualizations
  2. Applications to numerical approximation

The interactive graphs are created via Desmos applets, and math text is rendered via modern KaTeX math typesetting. We chose to include a handful of topics in numerical approximation to show some immediate payoff for the concepts learned, and because such topics also appear in future courses for mathematics, computer science, and engineering majors.

Numerical analysis is the cornerstone of many applications of mathematics, and we believe that applications are a great source of motivation and illustration for abstract mathematical concepts. It’s easier to understand concepts when you see them in live action — after all, calculus is the study of continuous change!

It is the understanding of concepts which we hope to enhance through this website. Techniques such as the chain rule and integration by parts are already covered in-depth in standard resources, and we choose not to repeat them here. We would have included proofs of why these techniques work, but decided against it because (1) they are readily accessible in open literature, and (2) we reasonably assume that such proofs would only be accessible to math majors, even if rigorous grounding benefits understanding for everyone.

We hope that our unique approach achieves our aim of presenting topics in calculus to a wide audience of students in a more digestible and motivated manner, as compared to how it has been traditionally taught.
