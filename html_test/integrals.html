The Lasallian's Guide to Calculus

Definite integrals

For someone acquainted with math history, it might seem strange that integral calculus always comes after differential calculus, because historically, the techniques associated with integration actually came first. The main idea behind integration, of calculating areas of shapes by first breaking them up into infinite sums of smaller shapes goes back as far as Archimedes calculating the volume of a sphere.

The idea of the definite integral is clear to any student: it is the area under the curve of a function. What may seem opaque is why derivatives and integrals are inverses of each other. This leads us to the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, which links the derivative and the definite integral. This answers our question of why differential calculus is always learned first, even though integration is historically an older method.

Theorem 4 (Fundamental Theorem of Calculus). If f ( x ) f(x) is continuous on a closed interval [ a , b ] [a,b] , and if F ( x ) F(x) is any antiderivative of f ( x ) f(x) ; i.e. F ′ ( x ) = f ( x ) ⇔ ∫ f ( x ) d x = F ( x ) , F'(x) = f(x) \quad \iff \quad \int f(x) \, dx = F(x), then ∫ a b f ( x ) d x = F ( b ) − F ( a ) . \int_a^b f(x) \, dx = F(b)-F(a).
